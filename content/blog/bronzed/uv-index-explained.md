Your weather app shows a number called UV. Most people never look at it. It's the one number on that screen that can actually hurt you.

Heat and ultraviolet radiation are separate things. A mild spring morning can carry more UV than a hot summer evening, because UV depends on the sun's angle, not the air temperature. Your skin only responds to UV. That's why the World Health Organization created a single scale for it: 0 to 11+, the same in Sydney, Miami, or Madrid.

## The scale, level by level

### 0–2: Low

Minimal risk for most people. Long hours outside still add up, and snow or water can nearly double your dose by reflection, but for a normal day you're fine.

### 3–5: Moderate

UV starts to matter here. Fair skin can begin to burn in 30 to 45 minutes. Sunscreen on exposed skin and some shade around midday are enough for most people.

### 6–7: High

Fair skin starts burning in 15 to 25 minutes. Broad-spectrum SPF 30+, a hat, and midday shade stop being optional. Most "I wasn't even out that long" sunburns happen in this range.

### 8–10: Very high

Damage is fast: 10 to 15 minutes for fair skin. The EPA's advice at this level is blunt: minimize sun between 10am and 4pm, reapply sunscreen every two hours, cover up. Beach days are the trap, since water and sand reflect UV back at you.

### 11+: Extreme

Unprotected skin burns in minutes. Shade, clothing, hat, sunglasses, sunscreen, and ideally a different plan. Move your outdoor time to early morning or evening.

## Same number, different skin

Two people sit in the same sun for the same hour. One turns pink, the other barely changes. Dermatologists describe this with the Fitzpatrick scale, six skin types sorted by how they respond to UV. Type I can burn in a fraction of the time Type VI needs.

So generic advice like "limit sun after 30 minutes" is nearly useless. The real answer to "how long can I stay out?" depends on three things: the UV level right now, your skin type, and your SPF.

## Five things that change your dose

1. **Time of day.** Roughly 60% of daily UV lands between 10am and 2pm. The same sky is UV 4 at noon and UV 2 at 9am.
2. **Clouds.** Up to 80% of UV passes through light cloud. Overcast days burn a lot of confident people.
3. **Reflection.** Snow can nearly double your dose. Sand adds up to 25%. Water reflects too.
4. **Altitude.** UV rises about 10% per 1,000 meters. Spring skiing can out-burn a beach day.
5. **Season and latitude.** UV peaks in summer and near the equator, but it never drops to zero.
