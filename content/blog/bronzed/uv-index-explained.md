Check the weather and you'll see it sitting quietly next to the temperature: a single number called the UV Index. It might be the most useful number on the whole screen, and most people never look at it.

Here's the thing about sunlight: the warmth you feel and the ultraviolet radiation hitting your skin are two different things. A mild, breezy spring day can carry more UV than a scorching summer evening. Your skin doesn't care what the thermometer says — it responds to UV. That's why the World Health Organization created the UV Index: one simple scale, from 0 to 11+, that tells you how strong the sun's UV radiation actually is right now.

## The scale, level by level

The UV Index was developed by the World Health Organization together with the World Meteorological Organization so that a "7" means the same thing in Sydney, Miami, or Madrid. Here's how to read it.

### 0–2: Low

Minimal risk for most people. You can generally be outside without much concern, though UV still adds up over long periods — and snow, water, and sand can nearly double exposure by reflection. If you burn very easily, a little protection never hurts.

### 3–5: Moderate

This is where UV starts to matter. Unprotected skin can begin to burn within about 30–45 minutes for fairer skin types. Sunscreen on exposed skin, sunglasses, and a bit of shade around midday are sensible defaults.

### 6–7: High

Protection is no longer optional. Fair skin can start burning in around 15–25 minutes. Broad-spectrum SPF 30+, a hat, and shade during the middle of the day make a real difference. This is also the range where a lot of "I wasn't even out that long" sunburns happen.

### 8–10: Very high

Skin damage happens fast — for fair skin types, in as little as 10–15 minutes. The EPA recommends minimizing sun exposure between 10am and 4pm at these levels, reapplying sunscreen every two hours, and covering up. Reflected UV from water and sand makes beach days at this level especially deceptive.

### 11+: Extreme

Unprotected skin can burn in minutes. Full protection — shade, clothing, hat, sunglasses, sunscreen — is the baseline, and the smartest move is simply to shift your outdoor plans to earlier or later in the day.

## Why the same number feels different on different skin

Two people can sit in the same sun, at the same UV level, for the same amount of time — and one goes pink while the other barely changes. Dermatologists describe this with the Fitzpatrick scale, which groups skin into six types based on how it responds to UV. Type I burns in a fraction of the time that Type V or VI does.

That's why generic advice like "limit sun after 30 minutes" is almost useless. The honest answer to "how long can I stay out?" is always: *it depends on the UV level right now, your skin type, and what you're wearing on your skin.*

## Five things that quietly change your UV exposure

1. **Time of day.** Roughly 60% of daily UV arrives between 10am and 2pm. The same afternoon UV 4 becomes a morning UV 2.
2. **Clouds are not sunscreen.** Up to 80% of UV passes through light cloud cover. Overcast days cause a surprising share of sunburns.
3. **Reflection.** Water, sand, and snow bounce UV back at you. Snow can nearly double your dose; sand adds up to 25%.
4. **Altitude.** UV increases about 10% with every 1,000 meters of elevation.
5. **Season and latitude.** UV peaks around midday in summer and closer to the equator, but "low season" doesn't mean zero — spring skiing at altitude can out-UV a summer beach day.

## Reading the number like a habit

The UV Index works best when checking it becomes automatic, like glancing at the temperature before choosing a jacket. A simple rule of thumb from the WHO: when the index is 3 or above, protection is worth it; when it's 8 or above, plan around the sun rather than against it.
