The UV Index and SPF answer different questions. The UV Index describes the strength of ultraviolet radiation at a place and time. SPF describes how a sunscreen performs in standardised testing when it is applied correctly. Neither number is a safe tanning countdown.

Use the UV Index to decide when protection is needed, then use sunscreen as one part of that protection.

## What the UV Index tells you

The UV Index starts at zero and has no upper limit. As it rises, the potential for skin and eye damage increases and harm can occur more quickly.

Australian guidance recommends sun protection when the UV Index is 3 or above. Temperature is not a substitute for this number: UV can be high when the air feels cool or the sky is overcast.

## What SPF tells you

SPF is based on the ratio between a UV dose received without sunscreen and the dose received through sunscreen that has been carefully applied. It does not mean you can multiply your usual burn time by the SPF and remain safely outside for that long.

Real use is affected by how much product is applied, missed areas, rubbing, perspiration, water, and time. ARPANSA states that no sunscreen provides complete protection and that its purpose is to reduce UV exposure, not extend time in the sun.

## Choose and apply sunscreen properly

ARPANSA and Cancer Council recommend a broad-spectrum, water-resistant SPF50 or SPF50+ sunscreen. Broad-spectrum products protect against both UVA and UVB.

For exposed skin:

- Apply sunscreen generously about 20 minutes before going outside.
- Reapply at least every two hours.
- Reapply after swimming, water sports, heavy sweating, or towel drying.
- Check the expiry date, follow the label, and store the product as directed.

Application matters. A high SPF cannot compensate for too little product or areas that were missed.

## Combine sunscreen with other protection

Do not rely on sunscreen alone. When UV is 3 or above, combine it with covering clothing, a broad-brimmed hat, shade, and protective sunglasses. Plan outdoor activities away from the strongest UV when practical.

A tan is evidence of UV damage. Sunscreen reduces exposure when used correctly; it does not make deliberate tanning safe.

## Use Bronzed without mistaking an estimate for safety

Bronzed considers live UV, weather, cloud cover, skin type, and a chosen SPF when producing a time estimate. An app cannot know exactly how much sunscreen you applied, whether it has rubbed off, how conditions have shifted, or how your skin will respond.

Treat the estimate as general information. Keep following official sun protection advice, respond to changing conditions, and leave the sun if you are concerned about your exposure.
