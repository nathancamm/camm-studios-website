The UV Index is a global scale for the strength of ultraviolet radiation at a specific place and time. It is not a temperature reading. A cool or cloudy day can still have high UV.

The number helps you decide when sun protection matters. The higher it climbs, the faster unprotected skin can be damaged.

## What the levels mean

### Low: 1 to 2

Protection is generally not required for most people during short outdoor periods. Reflective surfaces, altitude, medication, and personal sensitivity can still change your risk.

### Moderate: 3 to 5

Sun protection is recommended. Seek shade, wear protective clothing, use a broad brim hat and sunglasses, and apply broad spectrum sunscreen.

### High: 6 to 7

Skin damage can happen more quickly. Plan outdoor time with extra care and reduce exposure around the strongest part of the day.

### Very high: 8 to 10

Unprotected skin can be damaged quickly. Combine shade, clothing, a hat, sunglasses, and sunscreen.

### Extreme: 11 and above

UV is exceptionally strong. Minimize direct exposure and use every available form of protection.

## Why UV and heat feel different

Temperature describes how hot the air feels. UV measures radiation from the sun. They can move independently, which is why your senses cannot reliably judge UV exposure.

Cloud can reduce UV, but it does not always remove the need for protection. Altitude, reflection, season, time, and ozone also affect the level.

## What a tan means

A tan is a response to UV damage. There is no risk free way to tan through UV exposure. Bronzed can help you understand current conditions, but it cannot make tanning safe or prevent sunburn.

## Use the number as a decision signal

Check the forecast before you go outside, then keep watching conditions. When the UV Index reaches 3, follow official sun protection guidance. If you have a history of skin cancer, unusual sensitivity, or a health concern, ask a qualified clinician for advice.
